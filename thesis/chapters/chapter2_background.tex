\section{Emerging Research Topics and Conceptual Dimensions}
The phenomenon of technological and scientific emergence is characterized by dynamic, non-linear trajectories. As conceptualized by \citet{rotolo2015}, an emerging technology is defined by five central attributes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Radical Novelty:} The introduction of fundamentally distinct scientific principles, methods, or application domains.
    \item \textbf{Fast Growth:} Relatively rapid expansion in research activity, publications, patenting, or community participation compared to mature fields.
    \item \textbf{Coherence:} The formation of a distinct scientific identity, terminology, and interconnected community of practice.
    \item \textbf{Prominent Impact:} Substantial potential to influence scientific disciplines, industrial sectors, or societal challenges.
    \item \textbf{Uncertainty and Ambiguity:} High divergence regarding ultimate technological outcomes, commercial viable trajectories, and societal reception.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Bibliometrics and Scholarly Publication Indicators}
Bibliometric indicators provide empirical, quantitative records of scientific activity. Publication volume reflects the accumulation of scholarly effort, while citation metrics capture formal recognition and diffusion within the scientific network. In large-scale science studies, publication data derived from bibliographic databases allow continuous observation across thousands of research fields.

\section{National Scientific Specialisation and Economic Complexity}
National research systems develop differentiated research portfolios conditioned by historical path dependencies, institutional capacities, public funding incentives, and national industrial demands \citep{galanmena2026}. The economic complexity literature \citep{hidalgo2007} suggests that nations accumulate specific capabilities that facilitate diversification into related technological domains. In scientific publishing, national specialization captures whether a country concentrates a disproportionate share of its scientific effort in a particular topic relative to global norms.

\section{Revealed Comparative Advantage (RCA)}
Originally formulated in international trade economics by \citet{balassa1965}, the Revealed Comparative Advantage (RCA) index has been widely adapted in scientometrics to quantify relative national research intensity:
\begin{equation}
    RCA_{c,i,t} = \frac{P_{c,i,t} / P_{c,\text{All},t}}{P_{\text{Global},i,t} / P_{\text{Global},\text{All},t}}
\end{equation}
where $P_{c,i,t}$ is the publication output of country $c$ in topic $i$ at year $t$, $P_{c,\text{All},t}$ is the total publication output of country $c$ across all science, and $P_{\text{Global},i,t}$ and $P_{\text{Global},\text{All},t}$ are the corresponding global figures. An RCA value greater than 1.0 indicates that country $c$ has a revealed specialization in topic $i$ that exceeds the global baseline.

\section{Concepts in Quantitative Research Forecasting}
Research trend forecasting seeks to estimate the future growth, persistence, or decline of scientific topics. Methodologically, quantitative forecasting models range from simple extrapolation and autoregressive momentum baselines to regularized regression models and deep learning architectures \citep{liang2021}.
